§ 2

Background

Curve fitting — also called regression analysis or parameter estimation — is the process of constructing a mathematical model that best describes a set of measured data. It is one of the most fundamental quantitative techniques across scientific and engineering disciplines: from pharmacokinetics and enzyme kinetics in biology, to signal processing and materials characterisation in engineering, to cosmological parameter fitting in physics.

The core problem: given $n$ data pairs $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ and a parameterised model $f(x;\,\boldsymbol{p})$, find the parameter vector $\boldsymbol{p} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ that makes $f$ agree with the data as closely as possible in a well-defined sense. The standard criterion is ordinary least squares (OLS) — minimise the sum of squared residuals. For Gaussian measurement noise this coincides with maximum-likelihood estimation.

Linear models — where $f$ is linear in $\boldsymbol{p}$ — admit a closed-form analytic solution. Most physically motivated models (exponential decays, Gaussian peaks, Michaelis-Menten kinetics, logistic growth) are nonlinear in their parameters and require iterative numerical optimisation. This application implements four nonlinear solvers — Levenberg-Marquardt, Gauss-Newton, Nelder-Mead Simplex, and BFGS — selectable per fit, and analytic normal-equation solving for polynomials.

Local minima and initial values. The SSE landscape for nonlinear models is generally non-convex: multiple local minima may exist, and gradient-based solvers converge to whichever basin their starting point falls in. Poor initial parameter guesses can therefore produce meaningless results even when the algorithm reports convergence. The app provides data-driven Auto Init heuristics for every built-in model, and an optional multi-start strategy that launches several pilot runs from log-scale-perturbed starting points and polishes the best candidate — substantially reducing the risk of landing in a spurious local minimum.

Overfitting and model selection. A model with more free parameters will always achieve a lower SSE, but at the cost of fitting noise rather than signal. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) each penalise model complexity explicitly, allowing fair comparison across models of different sizes on the same dataset. Adjusted $R^2$ applies a degrees-of-freedom correction for the same reason. As a rule of thumb: prefer the simpler model whose AIC/BIC is within 2 units of the best-scoring model; a difference greater than 10 is considered very strong evidence in favour of the lower-scoring model.

Parameter identifiability. A parameter is identifiable if it can be uniquely recovered from noise-free data. Some models are structurally non-identifiable — for example, $y = (a \cdot b)\,e^{cx}$ conflates $a$ and $b$ into a single effective amplitude. Over-parameterised custom equations can exhibit the same pathology, manifesting as near-singular Jacobians and unreliable parameter standard errors. If standard errors are reported as NaN or are orders of magnitude larger than the parameter values, the model is likely non-identifiable given the available data.

Fully Local & Offline
All computation runs entirely in your browser. No data ever leaves your machine — no server, no cloud storage, no telemetry. The application continues to function without an internet connection once the page has loaded, making it safe for use with sensitive experimental datasets.
§ 3

Mathematical Formulation

The nonlinear least-squares objective to minimise over $\boldsymbol{p}$ is the sum of squared errors (SSE):

Objective: $S(\boldsymbol{p}) = \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i^2(\boldsymbol{p}),\quad r_i = y_i - f(x_i;\,\boldsymbol{p})$ minimise over $\boldsymbol{p}$
Jacobian: $J_{ij} = \dfrac{\partial r_i}{\partial p_j} \approx \dfrac{r_i(\boldsymbol{p}+h\,\mathbf{e}_j)-r_i(\boldsymbol{p})}{h}$ forward differences, $h = \max(\varepsilon|p_j|,\,\varepsilon)$
Normal eqs.: $\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\,\Delta\boldsymbol{p} = -\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{r}$ linearised (Gauss-Newton) step
$r_i = y_i - f(x_i;\boldsymbol{p})$ is the $i$-th residual; $\mathbf{J}$ is the $n\times m$ Jacobian of the residual vector ($J_{ij} = \partial r_i/\partial p_j \approx [r_i(\boldsymbol{p}+h\mathbf{e}_j)-r_i(\boldsymbol{p})]/h$); $\varepsilon = 10^{-7}$. The negative sign arises because $r = y - f$, so $\partial r_i/\partial p_j = -\partial f_i/\partial p_j$; equivalently, using the Jacobian of $f$ gives $\mathbf{J}_f^\top\mathbf{J}_f\,\Delta\boldsymbol{p} = \mathbf{J}_f^\top\mathbf{r}$ (positive sign, since $\mathbf{J}_f = -\mathbf{J}$).

The goodness-of-fit is characterised by several complementary statistics computed after convergence:

StatisticFormulaInterpretation
$R^2$$1 - \mathrm{SSE}\,/\,\mathrm{SST}$Fraction of variance explained (1 = perfect)
Adj. $R^2$$1-(1-R^2)\tfrac{n-1}{n-m-1}$$R^2$ penalised for model complexity
RMSE$\sqrt{\mathrm{SSE}/(n-m)}$Root-mean-square residual; same units as $y$
AIC$n\ln(\mathrm{SSE}/n)+2m$Information criterion — lower is better
BIC$n\ln(\mathrm{SSE}/n)+m\ln n$Stronger complexity penalty than AIC
SSE$\displaystyle\sum r_i^2$Absolute residual energy (scale-dependent)

where $\mathrm{SST} = \sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2$ is the total sum of squares and $m$ is the number of free parameters. Parameter standard errors are estimated from the covariance matrix $\mathbf{C} = \hat{\sigma}^2(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})^{-1}$, where $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \mathrm{SSE}/(n-m)$.

§ 4

Fitting Algorithms

Four solvers are available for nonlinear models. Polynomial models always use the analytic normal-equation path regardless of solver selection. The solver is chosen per-fit in the Algorithm Options panel.

Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) — the industry-standard gradient-based method. Interpolates between Gauss-Newton (fast near the minimum) and gradient descent (robust far from it) via a damping factor $\lambda$:

LM step: $\bigl(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J} + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})\bigr)\,\Delta\boldsymbol{p} = -\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{r}$ Marquardt scaling; $\mathbf{J}$ is Jacobian of residuals $r_i = y_i - f_i$
Update $\lambda$: $S_\text{new} < S \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda/3, \;\; \text{else} \;\; 10\lambda$ adaptive damping
Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$ is computed by forward finite differences ($\varepsilon = 10^{-7}$). Best for well-posed problems with good initial guesses. Recommended default.

Gauss-Newton (GN) — LM with $\lambda = 0$, plus a backtracking Armijo line search to guarantee descent. Faster convergence than LM when already near the minimum, but can diverge on poorly conditioned problems:

GN step: $\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\,\Delta\boldsymbol{p} = -\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{r}$ no damping ($\mathbf{J}$ = residual Jacobian)
Line search: $\alpha \leftarrow \alpha/2$ until $S(\boldsymbol{p}+\alpha\Delta\boldsymbol{p}) < S(\boldsymbol{p})$ Armijo backtracking
Use GN as a faster alternative to LM when you already have good initial guesses. Falls back gracefully via line search.

Nelder-Mead Simplex (NM) — derivative-free direct-search method. Maintains a simplex of $m+1$ vertices in parameter space and iteratively reflects, expands, or contracts it toward the minimum. Requires no Jacobian:

Reflect: $\boldsymbol{x}_r = \bar{\boldsymbol{x}} + \alpha(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}} - \boldsymbol{x}_\text{worst})$ $\alpha=1$
Expand: $\boldsymbol{x}_e = \bar{\boldsymbol{x}} + \gamma(\boldsymbol{x}_r - \bar{\boldsymbol{x}})$ $\gamma=2$, if $f_r < f_\text{best}$
Contract: $\boldsymbol{x}_c = \bar{\boldsymbol{x}} + \rho(\boldsymbol{x} - \bar{\boldsymbol{x}})$ $\rho=0.5$
Robust on noisy or non-smooth objectives where gradients are unreliable. Slower than gradient methods but does not require derivatives. Good when LM or GN give unexpected results.

BFGS (Quasi-Newton) — builds a low-rank approximation $\mathbf{H}_k$ to the inverse Hessian of $S$ from successive gradient evaluations, using the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno rank-2 update:

Direction: $\boldsymbol{d}_k = -\mathbf{H}_k\,\nabla S(\boldsymbol{p}_k)$ inverse-Hessian form
Update: $\mathbf{H}_{k+1} = \bigl(\mathbf{I} - \rho_k\boldsymbol{s}_k\boldsymbol{y}_k^\top\bigr)\mathbf{H}_k\bigl(\mathbf{I} - \rho_k\boldsymbol{y}_k\boldsymbol{s}_k^\top\bigr) + \rho_k\boldsymbol{s}_k\boldsymbol{s}_k^\top$ $\rho_k = 1/(\boldsymbol{y}_k^\top\boldsymbol{s}_k)$
where $\boldsymbol{s}_k = \boldsymbol{p}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{p}_k$ and $\boldsymbol{y}_k = \nabla S_{k+1}-\nabla S_k$. Gradients computed via finite differences of SSE. Curvature violations trigger a Hessian reset to identity. Typically superlinear convergence on smooth problems.

For polynomial models, the analytic least-squares solution is computed directly via the Vandermonde normal equations — exact, single-pass, no iteration required regardless of solver setting.

Solver Selection Guide
LM is the recommended default — robust, well-tested, and handles poor initial guesses. GN converges faster when already close to the solution. Nelder-Mead is useful when derivatives are unreliable or the surface is very flat. BFGS is effective on smooth problems with moderate numbers of parameters. All solvers benefit from good initial guesses — use Auto Init or tune manually.

§ 5

Using the Studio

Click Start Advanced Curve Fitting Studio on this page to open the full-screen app. On first launch a six-slide tutorial walks through the key features — dismiss it with Skip, or tick Don't show this again to suppress it on future visits. Load a dataset (Examples, Import, or Paste), select a model from the right panel, press ▶ Fit (or Ctrl+Enter), and read the diagnostics from the stats bar at the bottom. That's the entire core loop.

Three routes are available for loading data:

  • Examples — 16 built-in synthetic datasets with adjustable noise, covering every major model family. The dropdown is split into two columns: General (Exp Decay, Gaussian, Logistic Growth, etc.) and Calibration & Distributions / Electrophysiology (G-V Boltzmann, Kir I-V, HH Na Channel, voltage-dependent τ, and more). Each example pre-selects the matching model and sensible initial parameters. An Outliers slider and Outlier scale control let you inject random outliers to test robustness.
  • Import CSV / TSV / TXT — drag a file onto the plot area or use the Import button. Headers are optional; the first two numeric columns are used as X and Y. Comma, semicolon, tab, and space delimiters are all detected automatically. Files with more than two columns open a Column Picker where you choose X, Y, and optionally a σ (uncertainty) column. If a σ column is loaded, error bars appear on the scatter plot and the 1/σ² weighting option becomes available in Algorithm Options.
  • Paste Data — paste directly from Excel, a terminal, or any text editor. The delimiter auto-detection handles mixed formats.

Multiple datasets can coexist in one tab. Each is assigned a distinct colour, appears in the left panel, and can be independently enabled/disabled using the toggle button that appears on hover. Disabling a dataset hides it and all its associated fits from the plot, residual panels, stats table, and F-test — they are restored instantly when re-enabled.

The right panel lists 39 built-in models across 9 groups. A guide to choosing:

For iterative (nonlinear) models, the quality of the initial guess strongly affects convergence. Click Auto Init to run data-driven heuristics: amplitudes are estimated from the data range, rate constants from zero-crossing counts or log-linear regression, peak centres from the data maximum, and so on. If Auto Init produces a poor fit, inspect the parameter table and adjust manually — look at the plot to understand what each parameter physically controls. Each parameter also has a sweep slider directly below its row: drag it to preview the model curve in real time with no fitting. The slider updates the Init input live and draws a dashed amber preview line on the main plot, making it easy to build intuition about what each parameter controls and to narrow initial guesses visually before running the solver. Releasing the slider clears the preview. Enabling Multi-start (default: 8 pilots) perturbs the initial guess log-uniformly across an order of magnitude and picks the best result automatically, at the cost of ~4× compute time.

The parameter table shows four columns for each parameter. Init is the starting guess you provide; it is preserved across refits so you can always see where you started. Min and Max are optional box constraints — leave them blank for unconstrained, or enter a value to prevent the solver from crossing that boundary. All four solvers enforce bounds by projecting each candidate back into the feasible region at every iteration, and the initial guess is auto-clamped before dispatch. Typical uses: set Min = 0 for rates or amplitudes that cannot be negative, or Min = 0 / Max = 1 for fractions and probabilities. Fit shows the converged parameter value after the most recent run; hovering over a cell in that column shows the value with its ± standard error. Switching to a different fit in the fit list loads that fit's converged parameters into Init so you can re-run or modify it.

Each parameter row has a lock button (🔒) to the right. Clicking it freezes that parameter at its current Init value for the duration of the fit — the solver treats it as a constant. This is useful when one parameter is known from independent data (e.g. a fixed Hill coefficient, a known reversal potential, or a fixed baseline) and only the remaining parameters need optimisation. Locked parameters are shown in amber; clicking again unlocks them.

The Weights dropdown in Algorithm Options includes a Huber (IRLS) option for robust fitting. Standard least squares treats all residuals equally, making it sensitive to outliers. Huber IRLS (Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares) down-weights points with large residuals. At each of 5 reweighting iterations, the scale is estimated as MAD/0.6745 — the median absolute deviation scaled to be a consistent estimator of σ. This is deliberately chosen over RMSE because MAD is itself robust to outliers, so the threshold adapts correctly even when many points are contaminated. Points whose residuals fall within c = 1.345 standard deviations of this scale receive full weight; larger residuals are down-weighted proportionally. The result is a fit that minimises the influence of outliers without discarding them — compare the Huber fit against the OLS fit to quickly identify which points are pulling the curve.

The stats bar at the bottom shows all active fits side-by-side. Click any row to expand it — the row opens an inline detail panel showing every parameter with its fitted value, standard error, 95% confidence interval (t-distribution at the correct degrees of freedom), and t-statistic (green = statistically significant at the 95% level, amber = not significant). A right-hand column repeats the global summary stats at a larger, more readable size. Click the same row again to collapse it. Multiple rows can be expanded simultaneously and the bar scrolls.

Nonlinear fits run in a background Web Worker — the UI remains fully responsive while the solver iterates. Live progress (iteration count and current SSE) appears in the status bar. A red ✕ Cancel button is visible next to the Fit button while a fit is running; clicking it terminates the worker instantly. On browsers that block Workers (e.g. opening the file directly from disk), fitting falls back transparently to the main thread. The app validates your data before dispatching any fit: it checks for sufficient points, non-finite values, constant Y, and whether the model produces finite outputs at the initial parameters — plain-language errors are shown in the status bar so you know exactly what to fix.

The panel below the main plot has four tabs. Residuals shows $y_i - \hat{y}_i$ vs. $x_i$ for all fits whose source dataset is enabled; fits on disabled datasets are excluded entirely. Q-Q Plot plots sample residuals standardised by the fit RMSE against theoretical normal quantiles (Blom approximation) — points lying on the diagonal reference line indicate Gaussian-distributed residuals; systematic curvature reveals non-normality or outliers. Histogram shows the residual distribution with Sturges-rule binning and a fitted normal density overlay, providing a complementary view of the same information. Convergence plots SSE versus iteration; the default view uses a logarithmic Y axis (SSE) and a linear X axis (iterations). In-chart toggle buttons let you switch each axis independently between Log and Linear — useful for spotting early-iteration jumps on a linear scale or examining very fast convergence on a log X axis. For multi-start fits the pilot-selection phase and the final polish run share a monotonically increasing x-axis so you can see how much improvement the polishing step adds. The panel dims automatically while a fit is in progress. Enable Norm. Res. in the toolbar to switch residuals to σ units across all relevant tabs.

When the active data spans more than two decades (100×) on X or Y, a notification banner appears at the top of the main plot suggesting log axes with one-click apply buttons. Dismiss it with ✕ if not needed; it will not reappear until the session is reloaded.

Toggle the Residuals panel to inspect $y_i - \hat{y}_i$ vs. $x_i$. A good fit shows randomly scattered residuals around zero with no systematic trend. Curved residuals indicate the wrong model family. Fanning residuals (variance growing with $x$) suggest heteroscedasticity — consider log-transforming $y$ or switching to weighted fitting (1/y², 1/|y|, or 1/σ² if you have supplied uncertainties, selectable under Algorithm Options). Enable Norm. Res. in the toolbar to plot residuals in σ units (RMSE-normalised) instead of raw units — helpful for comparing residuals across fits with different scales.

Click CI Bands in the toolbar to overlay 95% confidence interval ribbons around each fit curve. The ribbon width reflects parameter uncertainty propagated through the Jacobian — narrow ribbons indicate a well-constrained fit; wide ribbons suggest under-determined parameters or sparse data. Click PI Bands to additionally show the wider 95% prediction interval ribbon, which adds per-observation scatter (RMSE²) to the parameter uncertainty. Use PI bands when you want to visualise where a new single measurement is likely to fall, rather than where the true mean curve lies.

Enable Outliers in the toolbar after fitting. Points where |residual| exceeds 2.5 × RMSE for the active fit are circled in red. The rings update live as you edit or move points. To exclude outliers from the fit entirely, click Mask 2.5σ in the Point Masking section of the right panel and refit — masked points remain visible on the plot but are excluded from the optimisation. Click Unmask All to restore them.

Click Data Table in the Point Masking section to open a scrollable table of every x/y pair in the active dataset, with the residual from the active fit shown for each point. A checkbox on each row lets you exclude that individual point from fitting while it remains visible on the plot as a hollow marker. Use Exclude 2.5σ to bulk-exclude all statistical outliers from the table, or Include All to restore the full dataset. This is the recommended way to handle a bad measurement point that should not be deleted entirely.

Click Pre-Process… in the Point Masking section to open the Pre-Process panel. It contains two independent sections, each with its own Apply button.

Smoothing Filter — select a method and click Apply Smoothing. Moving Average replaces each point with the unweighted mean of the surrounding window; simple and fast but blurs sharp features. Gaussian weights the window with a Gaussian kernel (controlled by σ in points) so that the center point contributes most — better shape preservation than a plain average. Savitzky-Golay fits a local polynomial (order 2–5) to each window by solving normal equations, which preserves peak heights and curvature far better than averaging; choose a higher polynomial order for signals with sharp peaks and a wide window for very noisy data. Median takes the window median rather than the mean, making it highly effective at removing isolated spike noise while keeping smooth regions and edges intact. In all methods, masked (excluded) points are skipped from the window and their values are left unchanged.

Fourier Filter — operates in the frequency domain using a native Cooley-Tukey FFT (the data is zero-padded to the next power-of-2 before transforming). Low-pass removes high-frequency noise to reveal a smooth underlying trend. High-pass removes slow baseline drift while keeping rapid variation. Band-pass passes only a specified frequency range, useful for isolating a known periodic signal. Notch (band-reject) zeros out a narrow band — useful for eliminating power-line interference or other periodic artefacts. Cutoffs are entered as a percentage of the Nyquist frequency (50% of sampling rate = 100% Nyquist). The Rolloff shape controls the transition: Brick-wall gives the sharpest possible cutoff but can introduce Gibbs ringing; Cosine taper and Hann window give progressively smoother roll-offs that trade transition-band width for lower ringing.

Click ▤ Show Spectrum to reveal an inline frequency analyser beneath the filter controls. Use the Spectrum / Spectrogram toggle to switch between two views. Spectrum shows the 1D power spectrum of the full dataset — frequency runs along the x-axis as a percentage of Nyquist and amplitude on the y-axis in dB (switch to linear with the dB checkbox). Frequencies that stand out at least 10 dB above the noise floor are automatically flagged with red ▾ markers labeled with their exact % Nyquist position, so contaminating tones are immediately visible without having to read individual peaks by eye. Red dashed vertical lines track the current cutoff positions as you type, letting you line them up with flagged peaks before committing. Spectrogram switches to an STFT heatmap: the dataset is divided into overlapping Hann-windowed frames, each is FFT'd, and the result is displayed as a 2D colour image (Viridis scale) with frequency on the x-axis and position along the dataset on the y-axis. Persistent sinusoidal interference appears as a sharp horizontal band at a constant frequency, while broadband noise fills the image uniformly — making the two visually unambiguous even when the 1D spectrum is crowded. Both views support scroll-to-zoom and click-drag-to-pan for close inspection. The cutoff lines carry over into spectrogram mode. The filter assumes approximately uniform x-axis spacing; masked-point status is preserved after filtering.

Click Restore Original in the footer of the Pre-Process panel at any time to revert to the exact imported y-values. Every Apply and Restore action is pushed onto the undo stack, so Ctrl+Z also steps back through pre-processing changes.

Click Try All in the toolbar to automatically fit every built-in model to the active dataset. A comparison table opens, ranked by R², showing R², RMSE, AIC, and BIC for each model. Click Apply on any row to load that model and its initial parameter estimates into the right panel for further tuning. Use AIC or BIC — rather than R² alone — to identify the best model without overfitting.

After fitting, click Copy (next to Auto Init in the Parameters section) to copy a plain-text summary to the clipboard. The output includes the fit name, model, dataset, all parameters with ± standard errors, and a full statistics block (R², Adj-R², RMSE, SSE, AIC, BIC, N, convergence status and iteration count).

By default fit curves are drawn only over the data X range. Set X min and X max in the Extrapolation Range section at the bottom of the right panel to extend (or restrict) the curve beyond the data. Click Reset to revert to the data range.

Point editing is always active — no mode button is needed. Click near any data point to select it; a translucent circle shows the current selection radius. Drag a selected point up or down to move it. Clicking and dragging away from all points pans the plot normally. Scroll to zoom. Hold Shift and scroll to adjust the multi-select radius. Press Ctrl+Z / Ctrl+Y to undo or redo edits, and arrow keys to nudge by the step amount. Click ✏ Edit in the toolbar to reveal the undo/redo buttons, step input, and radius display. A reset (⟳) button restores selected points to their original imported values.

Use the legend-icon button in the Plotly modebar (the toolbar that contains zoom, pan, and box-select) to toggle the legend on or off. The legend is draggable — click and drag it anywhere on the plot.

For custom equations, select Custom Equation from the model dropdown. Type any expression in the variable x using Math.js syntax. Parameters are any symbols other than x and standard math functions. Examples:

Double exp.:a * exp(-b*x) + c * exp(-d*x)
Power + offset:a * x^b + c
Stretched exp.:a * exp(-(x/tau)^beta)
Rational:(a*x + b) / (x^2 + c*x + d)
Supported functions include exp, log, sin, cos, sqrt, abs, atan, and all Math.js builtins. Use ^ or ** for powers.

The Predict / Solve panel (bottom of the right panel) has two modes. X → Y (predict): type any X value and press Go to evaluate the active fit at that point, reporting Ŷ with 95% CI half-width computed by Jacobian gradient propagation through the covariance matrix. Y → X (calibrate): type a target Y and press Go to find all X solutions numerically — a 500-point grid scan followed by bisection identifies every sign change in f(x) − Y over the curve range, and the CI on each solution is estimated via the delta method (δx ≈ CI_y / |df/dx|). This gives IC50, EC50, Km, half-life, and instrument calibration inverses directly from the fit with no post-processing.

The F-test panel compares two nested fits on the same dataset. Select fit A and fit B from the dropdowns (which auto-populate from the fit list); the simpler model is identified automatically as the null hypothesis. The panel reports the F-statistic, degrees of freedom (Δp, n − p₂), exact p-value from the F-distribution CDF, both SSE values, and a plain-English verdict. p < 0.05 means the extra parameters are statistically justified at α = 0.05.

The Annotations panel (just below Plot Labels in the right panel) adds overlay graphics directly to the Plotly figure. Click + Add to open the annotation editor and choose one of three types:

  • Horizontal line — a reference line at a fixed Y value (e.g., a detection limit, a half-maximum threshold). Extend across the full plot width.
  • Vertical line — a reference line at a fixed X value (e.g., a time point, a dose level, a phase boundary).
  • Text callout — free text anchored to any data-coordinate (X, Y) position, with an optional arrow pointing to the exact location.

Each annotation exposes full style controls: font family (9 presets including DM Sans, DM Mono, Arial, Helvetica, Times New Roman, Georgia, Courier New, Verdana, Trebuchet MS, or a custom string), size, bold, italic, and color; label horizontal anchor (left / center / right) and vertical anchor (top / middle / bottom); label background color + opacity and optional border; reference-line dash style (solid, dash, dot, dash-dot, long-dash, long-dash-dot), width, color, and opacity; and for text/peak callouts, arrowhead type (8 styles from filled triangle to open circle), size, line width, color, and pixel offset of the text from the arrow tail. Annotations are saved with the session and restore on tab switch or session load.

Click Peaks to automatically annotate the peak centre of every visible Gaussian or Lorentzian fit. Each auto-annotation is colored to match the fit curve, labelled with the fit name, and targeted to the peak centre parameter (μ or x₀). Re-clicking Peaks will not add duplicates.

Each tab is a fully independent workspace — opening a new tab starts with a clean slate (default model, no datasets, default solver settings, no annotations) with nothing inherited from other tabs. Tabs are auto-named from the first dataset loaded; double-click a tab name to rename it. Use Save Session to export one, some, or all tabs to a JSON file that can be reloaded later with Load Session. Enable Auto-restore to automatically reload the last saved session on page refresh; disable it to always start fresh.

§ 6

Built-in Model Reference

All built-in models, their equations, parameters, and physical interpretation. Polynomial models use analytic least-squares (Vandermonde normal equations — single pass, no iteration). All other models support four iterative solvers selectable in the Algorithm Options panel. Custom equations are parsed by Math.js — any symbol other than x and standard math functions is treated as a free parameter.

ModelEquationParametersUse Case & Notes
Linear$y = a\,x + b$a (slope), b (intercept)Calibration curves, proportional relationships.
Power Law$y = a\,|x|^b$a (amplitude), b (exponent)Allometric scaling ($b \approx 0.75$ in metabolic rate), fractal geometry, material hardness. Initial guess via log-linear regression.
Polynomial (2–6)$y = \sum_{k=0}^{n} c_k\,x^k$$n+1$ coefficients $c_0\ldots c_n$General smooth nonlinearity. Use AIC/BIC to select degree — higher is not always better. Analytic Vandermonde solution.
Exponential$y = a\,e^{bx}$a (pre-factor), b (rate)Unrestricted growth ($b>0$) or decay ($b<0$). First model to try for monotone data crossing zero.
Exp Decay + Offset$y = a\,e^{-bx} + c$a (amplitude), b (rate), c (offset)Radioactive decay to background, RC circuit discharge, first-order chemical reaction. $c$ absorbs baseline.
Logistic / Sigmoid$y = \dfrac{L}{1+e^{-k(x-x_0)}}$L (upper asymptote), k (steepness), x₀ (midpoint)Population growth with carrying capacity, technology adoption, dose-response without cooperativity. $x_0$ is the EC50.
Gaussian Peak$y = A\,e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}+C$A (height), μ (centre), σ (width), C (baseline)Spectroscopic peaks, chromatography, probability density. FWHM $= 2\sqrt{2\ln 2}\,\sigma \approx 2.355\,\sigma$.
Lorentzian Peak$y = A\dfrac{\gamma^2}{(x-x_0)^2+\gamma^2}+C$A (height), x₀ (centre), γ (half-width), C (baseline)NMR and optical resonance lineshapes, homogeneous broadening. Heavier tails than Gaussian. FWHM $= 2\gamma$.
Michaelis-Menten$y = \dfrac{V_{\max}\,x}{K_m+x}$Vmax (saturation), Km (half-sat. conc.)Enzyme kinetics: $V_{\max}$ is maximum reaction rate; $K_m$ is the substrate concentration at half-max rate. Also Langmuir adsorption isotherm.
Hill Equation$y = \dfrac{V_{\max}\,x^n}{K_d^n+x^n}$Vmax, Kd (EC50), n (Hill coefficient)Cooperative ligand binding, dose-response curves. $n=1$ reduces to Michaelis-Menten; $n>1$ indicates positive cooperativity; $n<1$ indicates negative cooperativity.
Sinusoidal$y = A\sin(\omega x+\phi)+C$A (amplitude), ω (angular freq.), φ (phase), C (offset)Periodic oscillations, seasonal time series, AC signal characterisation. Period $T = 2\pi/\omega$.
Damped Sinusoid$y = A\,e^{-\gamma x}\sin(\omega x+\phi)+C$A, γ (decay rate), ω, φ, CFree vibration of under-damped systems, RLC transient, acoustic decay. Quality factor $Q = \omega/(2\gamma)$.
Weibull CDF$y = 1 - e^{-(x/\lambda)^k}$λ (scale / characteristic life), k (shape)Reliability and survival analysis. $k<1$: infant mortality; $k=1$: exponential (random failure); $k>1$: wear-out. $\lambda$ is the 63.2th-percentile lifetime.
ELECTROPHYSIOLOGY
Boltzmann G-V$G = \dfrac{A}{1+e^{-(V-V_h)/k}}$A (max conductance), Vh (half-activation mV), k (slope factor mV)Voltage-gated channel activation or inactivation G-V curve. Vh is the half-activation voltage; positive k gives activation, negative k gives inactivation.
Double Boltzmann$G = \dfrac{A_1}{1+e^{-(V-V_{h1})/k_1}} + \dfrac{A_2}{1+e^{-(V-V_{h2})/k_2}}$A1, Vh1, k1, A2, Vh2, k2Two-component G-V (e.g. two channel populations, two voltage sensors, or overlapping activation/inactivation). Six parameters; use AIC to verify the extra component is justified.
HH Activation I-V$I = g\cdot m_\infty(V)^p\cdot(V-E_\text{rev})$, $m_\infty = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-(V-V_m)/k_m}}$g (max conductance), Vm (half-act. mV), km (slope mV), p (gate order), Erev (reversal potential mV)Hodgkin-Huxley-type I-V for a single activation gate of order p (typically 2–4). Suitable for delayed-rectifier K⁺ or L-type Ca²⁺ channels.
HH Na Channel I-V$I = g\cdot m_\infty^3\cdot h_\infty\cdot(V-E_\text{rev})$g, Vm, km, Vh (half-inact. mV), kh (inact. slope mV), ErevClassic HH sodium channel with cubic activation gate (m³) and linear inactivation gate (h). The inactivation Boltzmann uses a sign-reversed exponent: $h_\infty = 1/(1+e^{+(V-V_h)/k_h})$.
Kir Inward Rectifier$I = g\cdot\dfrac{V-E_K}{1+e^{(V-V_h)/k}}$g (conductance), EK (reversal mV), Vh (rectification mV), k (slope mV)Inward-rectifier K⁺ channel (Kir2.x). The Boltzmann denominator captures the strong inward rectification; Vh sets the voltage at which rectification is half-maximal.
GHK Current$I = A\cdot\dfrac{V(1-r\,e^{-V/V_t})}{1-e^{-V/V_t}}$A (permeability × conc. factor), r = [ion]_out/[ion]_in, Vt (thermal voltage ≈ 25.7 mV)Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz constant-field current equation. Accounts for asymmetric ion concentrations across the membrane. L'Hôpital limit applied analytically at V = 0 to avoid singularity.
τ-V Gaussian$\tau = \tau_\text{max}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{V-V_\text{peak}}{k}\right)^2} + \tau_\text{min}$τmax (peak time constant ms), Vpeak (peak voltage mV), k (width mV), τmin (baseline ms)Bell-shaped voltage dependence of a gating time constant, as observed for HH m and h gates. τmin sets the asymptotic floor at extreme voltages.
GENERAL (EXTENDED)
Double-Gaussian Peak$y = A_1\,e^{-(x-\mu_1)^2/2\sigma_1^2} + A_2\,e^{-(x-\mu_2)^2/2\sigma_2^2} + C$A1, μ1, σ1 (first peak), A2, μ2, σ2 (second peak), C (baseline)Overlapping spectroscopic peaks, bimodal distributions, doublet resonances. Use AIC to verify the second component is justified vs. a single Gaussian.
Biexponential$y = A_1\,e^{-k_1 x} + A_2\,e^{-k_2 x} + C$A1, k1 (fast component), A2, k2 (slow component), C (offset)Two-compartment pharmacokinetics, fluorescence decay with two lifetime components, parallel first-order reactions. The two rate constants k1 and k2 capture fast and slow processes simultaneously.
Rational (1,1)$y = \dfrac{p_0 + p_1\,x}{1 + q_1\,x}$p0 (numerator intercept), p1 (numerator slope), q1 (denominator slope)Padé approximant for functions with a horizontal asymptote; enzyme inhibition with substrate excess; adsorption isotherms with saturation. More flexible than a hyperbola for asymmetric data.
Power Law + Offset$y = a\,x^b + c$a (amplitude), b (exponent), c (baseline offset)Power law with a non-zero floor; material strength–thickness relationships, electrode impedance scaling. The offset c prevents the curve from passing through zero, unlike the basic power law.
GROWTH / DECAY (EXTENDED)
Gompertz$y = A\,e^{-e^{-k(x-x_0)}}$A (carrying capacity), k (growth rate), x₀ (inflection point)Asymmetric sigmoid — saturates more slowly than logistic. Tumor volume growth, bacterial growth curves, technology adoption, insect population dynamics. Inflection at y = A/e ≈ 0.368 A.
KWW (Stretched Exp)$y = A\,e^{-(x/\tau)^\beta}+C$A (amplitude), τ (characteristic time), β (stretch exponent, 0–1), C (baseline)Kohlrausch-Williams-Watts stretched exponential. β = 1 is ordinary exponential; β < 1 gives subexponential (dispersive) decay. Dielectric relaxation in glasses, polymer chain dynamics, fluorescence afterglow, battery capacity fade. Also written as exp(-(t/τ)^β).
PEAK / SPECTRAL (EXTENDED)
Pseudo-Voigt$y = A\left[\eta\dfrac{\gamma^2}{(x-x_0)^2+\gamma^2}+(1-\eta)\,e^{-(x-x_0)^2/2\sigma^2}\right]+C$A (amplitude), x₀ (center), γ (Lorentz HWHM), σ (Gauss width), η (mixing, 0–1), C (baseline)Weighted sum of Lorentzian and Gaussian approximating the true Voigt convolution to <1% error. Standard in X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman, IR, neutron scattering. η → 1 approaches pure Lorentzian; η → 0 pure Gaussian. More physically meaningful than fitting either lineshape alone.
Fano Resonance$y = A\dfrac{(q+\varepsilon)^2}{1+\varepsilon^2}+C,\;\varepsilon=\dfrac{x-x_0}{\Gamma}$A (amplitude), x₀ (resonance center), Γ (linewidth), q (asymmetry / Fano parameter), C (background)Asymmetric lineshape arising from quantum interference between a discrete resonance and a continuum. Seen in nanophotonic/plasmonic structures, NEMS resonators, Raman spectra near electronic resonances, photonic crystals, atomic physics. q = 0 gives a symmetric dip (anti-resonance); large |q| approaches a symmetric peak.
BIOLOGY / PHARMACOLOGY (EXTENDED)
4-Parameter Logistic (4PL)$y = D + \dfrac{A-D}{1+(x/C)^B}$A (top asymptote), D (bottom asymptote), C (EC50/IC50), B (Hill slope)Standard model for ELISA, immunoassays, flow cytometry, and dose-response. Unlike Logistic and Hill (which force the lower asymptote to 0), 4PL has free upper and lower plateaux. C is the inflection point (EC50 or IC50); B is the Hill slope (steepness). Reduces to Hill equation when D = 0.
Oral 1-Compartment PK$C = \dfrac{\mathrm{Amp}\cdot k_a}{k_a-k_e}(e^{-k_e t}-e^{-k_a t})$Amp (dose factor = F·D/V), ka (absorption rate h⁻¹), ke (elimination rate h⁻¹)Drug concentration-time profile after oral administration. Rising absorption phase (ka) followed by exponential elimination (ke). Peak concentration at T_max = ln(ka/ke)/(ka−ke). L'Hôpital limit applied analytically when ka ≈ ke. Amp absorbs bioavailability, dose, and volume of distribution.
Stern-Volmer$F = \dfrac{F_0}{(1+K_D[Q])(1+K_S[Q])}$F₀ (unquenched fluorescence), KD (dynamic quenching constant M⁻¹), KS (static quenching constant M⁻¹)Combined static and dynamic fluorescence quenching. Dynamic quenching (KS = 0) gives a linear Stern-Volmer plot; static + dynamic gives upward curvature. Used in biosensor characterisation, protein-ligand binding studies, FRET, and collisional quenching assays. Setting KS = 0 recovers the classical linear form F₀/F = 1 + Ksv[Q].
PHYSICS / MATERIAL
Langevin$y = A\!\left(\coth(Bx)-\dfrac{1}{Bx}\right)$A (saturation value Ms), B (Langevin parameter ∝ μ/kT)Magnetization of a classical paramagnet or superparamagnetic nanoparticle vs. applied field H. A = saturation magnetization Ms; B = μ/(kBT) where μ is the particle moment. Also describes polymer chain extension (freely-jointed chain model) and orientational polarization in dielectrics. L'Hôpital limit applied at Bx ≈ 0 to avoid 0/0.
Van't Hoff$\ln K = \dfrac{\Delta S}{R} - \dfrac{\Delta H}{RT}$, i.e. $K = e^{\Delta S/R - \Delta H/(RT)}$dHR = ΔH/R (K), dSR = ΔS/R (dimensionless); x = T (Kelvin)Equilibrium constant K vs. absolute temperature T. Linear in a ln K vs. 1/T (Van't Hoff plot) for constant ΔH; the nonlinear form here fits K vs. T directly. Used for protein folding/unfolding thermodynamics, binding affinity temperature dependence, solubility and reaction equilibria. Auto-init uses log-linear regression on ln K vs. 1/T.
Ramberg-Osgood$\varepsilon = \dfrac{\sigma}{E} + \left(\dfrac{|\sigma|}{K}\right)^{1/n}$E (Young's modulus, elastic stiffness), K (strength coefficient), n (hardening exponent)Smooth elastic-plastic stress-strain relationship. The linear term σ/E is elastic; the power-law term captures work hardening. Common for metals (aluminium alloys, steels) and polymers above the yield point. n ≈ 5–20 for most metals; larger n → sharper yield transition. Sign-preserving: works for both tensile (σ > 0) and compressive (σ < 0) loading.
