§ 1

Background

Curve fitting — also called regression analysis or parameter estimation — is the process of constructing a mathematical model that best describes a set of measured data. It is one of the most fundamental quantitative techniques across scientific and engineering disciplines: from pharmacokinetics and enzyme kinetics in biology, to signal processing and materials characterisation in engineering, to cosmological parameter fitting in physics.

The core problem: given $n$ data pairs $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ and a parameterised model $f(x;\,\boldsymbol{p})$, find the parameter vector $\boldsymbol{p} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ that makes $f$ agree with the data as closely as possible in a well-defined sense. The standard criterion is ordinary least squares (OLS) — minimise the sum of squared residuals. For Gaussian measurement noise this coincides with maximum-likelihood estimation.

Linear models — where $f$ is linear in $\boldsymbol{p}$ — admit a closed-form analytic solution. Most physically motivated models (exponential decays, Gaussian peaks, Michaelis-Menten kinetics, logistic growth) are nonlinear in their parameters and require iterative numerical optimisation. This application implements four nonlinear solvers — Levenberg-Marquardt, Gauss-Newton, Nelder-Mead Simplex, and BFGS — selectable per fit, and analytic normal-equation solving for polynomials.

Fully Local & Offline
All computation runs entirely in your browser. No data ever leaves your machine — no server, no cloud storage, no telemetry. The application continues to function without an internet connection once the page has loaded, making it safe for use with sensitive experimental datasets.
§ 2

Mathematical Formulation

The nonlinear least-squares objective to minimise over $\boldsymbol{p}$ is the sum of squared errors (SSE):

Objective: $S(\boldsymbol{p}) = \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i^2(\boldsymbol{p}),\quad r_i = y_i - f(x_i;\,\boldsymbol{p})$ minimise over $\boldsymbol{p}$
Jacobian: $J_{ij} = \dfrac{\partial r_i}{\partial p_j} \approx \dfrac{r_i(\boldsymbol{p}+h\,\mathbf{e}_j)-r_i(\boldsymbol{p})}{h}$ forward differences, $h = \max(\varepsilon|p_j|,\,\varepsilon)$
Normal eqs.: $\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\,\Delta\boldsymbol{p} = -\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{r}$ linearised (Gauss-Newton) step
$r_i = y_i - f(x_i;\boldsymbol{p})$ is the $i$-th residual; $\mathbf{J}$ is the $n\times m$ Jacobian of the residual vector ($J_{ij} = \partial r_i/\partial p_j \approx [r_i(\boldsymbol{p}+h\mathbf{e}_j)-r_i(\boldsymbol{p})]/h$); $\varepsilon = 10^{-7}$. The negative sign arises because $r = y - f$, so $\partial r_i/\partial p_j = -\partial f_i/\partial p_j$; equivalently, using the Jacobian of $f$ gives $\mathbf{J}_f^\top\mathbf{J}_f\,\Delta\boldsymbol{p} = \mathbf{J}_f^\top\mathbf{r}$ (positive sign, since $\mathbf{J}_f = -\mathbf{J}$).

The goodness-of-fit is characterised by several complementary statistics computed after convergence:

StatisticFormulaInterpretation
$R^2$$1 - \mathrm{SSE}\,/\,\mathrm{SST}$Fraction of variance explained (1 = perfect)
Adj. $R^2$$1-(1-R^2)\tfrac{n-1}{n-m-1}$$R^2$ penalised for model complexity
RMSE$\sqrt{\mathrm{SSE}/(n-m)}$Root-mean-square residual; same units as $y$
AIC$n\ln(\mathrm{SSE}/n)+2m$Information criterion — lower is better
BIC$n\ln(\mathrm{SSE}/n)+m\ln n$Stronger complexity penalty than AIC
SSE$\displaystyle\sum r_i^2$Absolute residual energy (scale-dependent)

where $\mathrm{SST} = \sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2$ is the total sum of squares and $m$ is the number of free parameters. Parameter standard errors are estimated from the covariance matrix $\mathbf{C} = \hat{\sigma}^2(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})^{-1}$, where $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \mathrm{SSE}/(n-m)$.

§ 3

Fitting Algorithms

Four solvers are available for nonlinear models. Polynomial models always use the analytic normal-equation path regardless of solver selection. The solver is chosen per-fit in the Algorithm Options panel.

Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) — the industry-standard gradient-based method. Interpolates between Gauss-Newton (fast near the minimum) and gradient descent (robust far from it) via a damping factor $\lambda$:

LM step: $\bigl(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J} + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J})\bigr)\,\Delta\boldsymbol{p} = -\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{r}$ Marquardt scaling; $\mathbf{J}$ is Jacobian of residuals $r_i = y_i - f_i$
Update $\lambda$: $S_\text{new} < S \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda/3, \;\; \text{else} \;\; 10\lambda$ adaptive damping
Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$ is computed by forward finite differences ($\varepsilon = 10^{-7}$). Best for well-posed problems with good initial guesses. Recommended default.

Gauss-Newton (GN) — LM with $\lambda = 0$, plus a backtracking Armijo line search to guarantee descent. Faster convergence than LM when already near the minimum, but can diverge on poorly conditioned problems:

GN step: $\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{J}\,\Delta\boldsymbol{p} = -\mathbf{J}^\top\mathbf{r}$ no damping ($\mathbf{J}$ = residual Jacobian)
Line search: $\alpha \leftarrow \alpha/2$ until $S(\boldsymbol{p}+\alpha\Delta\boldsymbol{p}) < S(\boldsymbol{p})$ Armijo backtracking
Use GN as a faster alternative to LM when you already have good initial guesses. Falls back gracefully via line search.

Nelder-Mead Simplex (NM) — derivative-free direct-search method. Maintains a simplex of $m+1$ vertices in parameter space and iteratively reflects, expands, or contracts it toward the minimum. Requires no Jacobian:

Reflect: $\boldsymbol{x}_r = \bar{\boldsymbol{x}} + \alpha(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}} - \boldsymbol{x}_\text{worst})$ $\alpha=1$
Expand: $\boldsymbol{x}_e = \bar{\boldsymbol{x}} + \gamma(\boldsymbol{x}_r - \bar{\boldsymbol{x}})$ $\gamma=2$, if $f_r < f_\text{best}$
Contract: $\boldsymbol{x}_c = \bar{\boldsymbol{x}} + \rho(\boldsymbol{x} - \bar{\boldsymbol{x}})$ $\rho=0.5$
Robust on noisy or non-smooth objectives where gradients are unreliable. Slower than gradient methods but does not require derivatives. Good when LM or GN give unexpected results.

BFGS (Quasi-Newton) — builds a low-rank approximation $\mathbf{H}_k$ to the inverse Hessian of $S$ from successive gradient evaluations, using the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno rank-2 update:

Direction: $\boldsymbol{d}_k = -\mathbf{H}_k\,\nabla S(\boldsymbol{p}_k)$ inverse-Hessian form
Update: $\mathbf{H}_{k+1} = \bigl(\mathbf{I} - \rho_k\boldsymbol{s}_k\boldsymbol{y}_k^\top\bigr)\mathbf{H}_k\bigl(\mathbf{I} - \rho_k\boldsymbol{y}_k\boldsymbol{s}_k^\top\bigr) + \rho_k\boldsymbol{s}_k\boldsymbol{s}_k^\top$ $\rho_k = 1/(\boldsymbol{y}_k^\top\boldsymbol{s}_k)$
where $\boldsymbol{s}_k = \boldsymbol{p}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{p}_k$ and $\boldsymbol{y}_k = \nabla S_{k+1}-\nabla S_k$. Gradients computed via finite differences of SSE. Curvature violations trigger a Hessian reset to identity. Typically superlinear convergence on smooth problems.

For polynomial models, the analytic least-squares solution is computed directly via the Vandermonde normal equations — exact, single-pass, no iteration required regardless of solver setting.

Solver Selection Guide
LM is the recommended default — robust, well-tested, and handles poor initial guesses. GN converges faster when already close to the solution. Nelder-Mead is useful when derivatives are unreliable or the surface is very flat. BFGS is effective on smooth problems with moderate numbers of parameters. All solvers benefit from good initial guesses — use Auto Init or tune manually.
